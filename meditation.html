Letter to a Friend: The Danger of Seeking Bliss

Sorry it took me a while to respond to your email. I'm not familiar with Turiya state so I can't comment on it. However, since the class reached that state through guided meditation then I would suspect it is achieved through the power of suggestion.

I'm skeptical of any meditation that is not observing the reality of the present as is, whether one is experiencing a pleasant or an unpleasant mental state, with full acceptance. True happiness and compassion arise from experiencing suffering and letting it go since everything is impermanent; thus in essence there is no self as it is also impermanent and subject to change.

Suffering is caused by the attachment to anything at all. Since all things are impermanent, therefore attachment to anything at all will lead to suffering. For example, a rose is beautiful and fragrant while in full bloom, yet it will not last and the attachment to that state in its development will lead to suffering. The greater the attachment, the greater the suffering.

Trying to reach a Turiya state for its blissful effect will hinder one's progress in meditation. The goal of meditation is to move beyond suffering, which is not the same as trying to experience bliss. One can take drugs to feel good but it doesn't lead to greater wisdom and compassion, only more suffering.

Sincerely,
Tuan Tran

My Spiritual Journey: From Saigon to Myanmar

I have been drawn to meditation since I was a boy. At twelve years old I tried to meditate on the breath by myself, without instruction, without a teacher.

In 1986, at twenty-six, I attended my first Goenka course and reached bhanga during that retreat. It transformed my life. I traveled to Dhamma Giri in India seeking deeper guidance from S.N. Goenka himself.

I met with him and expressed my desire to deepen my practice through intensive meditation in solitude to work with what I had experienced. I asked if I could use one of the meditation cells at the center to practice alone, without the recorded instructions and group schedule.

He told me: continue with the ten-day course and the recorded instructions, or leave.

I left. I did not find what I was looking for then.

In 2010, at fifty, I became a samanera at Pa Auk Forest Monastery in Myanmar. I woke each morning to the sound of the temple bell. Fourteen hours of meditation a day. I wanted to stay for the rest of my life.

My stay was not long enough to reach jhāna, or even the nimitta. A message arrived from my wife. Our son needed his father. The Buddha taught that attachment is the root of suffering, but he also taught that compassion is the highest virtue, and compassion is not a feeling. It is packing your bag when you want to stay.

I now meditate daily on the breath. I carry that awareness through the day.

In 2026, I found what I had been looking for in a book about the origins of the Goenka method. Anāgām Sayāgyi U Thet and His Teaching documents how the body-scanning technique arose from one lay farmer's own meditative experience in early twentieth-century Burma, a discovery of a sincere practitioner, not a technique handed down unchanged from the Buddha. Understanding this freed me. The breath method I had found at twelve had been complete all along.

The Webu Sayadaw Method: Direct Path

Webu Sayadaw (1896–1977) was one of Burma's most revered meditation masters, renowned for the uncompromising simplicity of his teaching. While others emphasized extensive scriptural study, he insisted that liberation could be reached through one thing alone, unwavering attention to the breath. He is widely credited with preserving mindfulness of breathing as a living practice at a time when it had nearly disappeared from the tradition, and among his admirers were some of the most influential teachers of the modern era.

I believe Webu Sayadaw's method, mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati), is the most direct path. It is a complete method for developing both concentration and wisdom: observe the natural breath at the nostrils, where the air touches, without controlling it, and with continuous awareness.

Know that you are breathing in while breathing in; know that you are breathing out while breathing out; and be aware of the spot at the nostrils where the stream of air touches as you breathe in and out.

The mind has a tendency to wander, either thinking about the past or the future. Accept that at this moment the mind is wandering and patiently bring the mind back to being aware of the breath at the touching point. This is the process of training the mind to be in the present moment.

When the breath becomes so subtle that it seems no longer to touch, it is in fact still touching. Keep your attention fixed on the spot whether or not you can perceive the sensation of contact.

This is the complete method. Follow it precisely and practice diligently. Any question you have will reveal its answer through your own sustained practice.

Those who wish to hear his teaching in his own words can read his discourses, collected in The Way to Ultimate Calm: Selected Discourses of Webu Sayadaw (Buddhist Publication Society), and freely distributed by the International Meditation Centre in the tradition of Sayagyi U Ba Khin: IMC Publications →

Webu Sayadaw and Sayagyi U Ba Khin were close Dhamma friends from their first meeting in 1941. It was Webu Sayadaw who, upon examining U Ba Khin's practice, urged him to begin teaching, even though the method U Ba Khin had learned from Saya Thet Gyi was not the method Webu Sayadaw himself taught. He taught only this: the breath at the touching point, continuous awareness, nothing else. The Buddha taught forty methods of meditation, and body scanning is not one of them.

Modern scholarship supports the same conclusion. In The Birth of Insight: Meditation, Modern Buddhism, and the Burmese Monk Ledi Sayadaw (University of Chicago Press, 2013), Erik Braun traces how Ledi Sayadaw, responding to British colonialism's threat to Burmese Buddhism, took meditation, traditionally a monastic practice, and made it accessible to lay householders, launching the mass lay meditation movement that would eventually spread worldwide through Mahasi Sayadaw, U Ba Khin, and S.N. Goenka. The distinctive techniques that became fixed as "the" Vipassana method took shape within this modern movement, formed by the circumstances and innovations of twentieth-century teachers. Braun also notes the striking absence at the tradition's root: the claim of a direct transmission from the Buddha through the lineage is something Ledi Sayadaw himself never made. What the tradition presents as preservation, the history reveals as adaptation.

Bhikkhu Anālayo, a scholar-monk trained in the early Pāli texts, addressed the question directly in his article "Buddhist Antecedents to the Body Scan Meditation" (Mindfulness, vol. 11, 2020). Tracing the body scan through the Burmese Vipassana tradition, he concludes that the body scan as such "would presumably have been unknown in early Buddhism" — that the practice developed out of a particular modern interpretation of the third step in the canonical instructions on mindfulness of breathing ("experiencing the whole body"), rather than constituting a technique the Buddha himself taught. His earlier book, Satipaṭṭhāna: The Direct Path to Realization (Windhorse, 2003), reaches the same conclusion through study of the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta and its parallels: the Buddha's instructions describe the breath, postures, activities, anatomical parts, elements, and decaying corpses, but nowhere a systematic sweep of attention through the body. Two researchers working independently, one through history and one through textual study, arrive at the same finding. A third, working through the surviving meditation manuals of precolonial Southeast Asia, confirms what they found.

Kate Crosby's Esoteric Theravada: The Story of the Forgotten Meditation Tradition of Southeast Asia (Shambhala, 2020) confirms this. She documents the boran kammatthana ("traditional" or "old" meditation) systems that prevailed across Southeast Asia for centuries before the modern reforms: elaborate practices built around the breath, its nimittas, and the whole body's signs of refinement. These were the methods displaced, not preserved, by the twentieth-century movements that gave us the body scan. The breath method of Webu Sayadaw and the lineage of U Ba Khin simplified what remained of an older, richer curriculum, but the breath itself was never the innovation. Webu Sayadaw gave us the pure, stripped-down breath method. Crosby shows us that this method wasn't invented in the twentieth century; it was the core of an older and broader tradition that the modern body-scan movement bypassed.
